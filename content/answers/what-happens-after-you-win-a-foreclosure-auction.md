The clerk files a certificate of sale right after the auction, opening a 10-day window for anyone with an interest in the property to object. If nobody objects, the clerk files a certificate of title in your name — that filing, not the winning bid, is when you actually own the property. Everything after that is yours to run.

## The clerk's side: sale, objection window, title

A Florida foreclosure sale doesn't hand over ownership at the courthouse steps or in the online bid window. The clerk files a certificate of sale, then holds the file open for objections. If none arrive, the clerk files a certificate of title and serves it on each party — that filing is the transfer. Until it happens, treat the win as pending, not owned.

## Where bidders lose money between the gavel and the title

The pain isn't the paperwork — it's what happens to your plan while you wait on it. A bidder who wins at the sale and has no budget, no scope of work, and no schedule ready loses days re-typing the deal into a spreadsheet or a second tool instead of moving. A tax deed bought for a few thousand dollars turns into a sale in the high six figures because the budget, the draws, and the schedule were already set before the certificate cleared — not improvised after.

A BidDeed Project carries the deal from the auction calendar through the sale: budget and scopes of work, a schedule, a ledger with receipts and budget-vs-actual, lender draws, a funding pack built from the SIGNAL$ Property Report, and an exit pack when it's time to sell or rent.
